The first referral is the easy part. Keeping a physician, a psychiatrist or a fellow therapist thinking of you six months later is where most referral networks quietly fall apart. When I managed referral relationships in health care, more than 300 of them at one point, the difference between a contact and a steady source was almost never charisma. It was follow-through, and follow-through only happens when there's a system.

Here's the one I'd set up for any private practice.

## 1. Decide who belongs in your network

Start with three groups:

- **People who see your clients before you do.** Primary care, OB-GYNs, pediatricians, school counselors, physical therapists, specialists whose patients live with the issues you treat.
- **People who need somewhere to send clients after them.** Psychiatric prescribers, psychologists who do testing, intensive outpatient programs stepping clients down to weekly care.
- **Other therapists.** Colleagues with complementary specialties, full caseloads, or a different insurance mix. A clinician who doesn't see couples needs a couples therapist to send people to, and you need one for the individual work you don't do.

A list of 40 to 60 well-chosen names is plenty for a solo practice. More than that and you can't keep up the cadence that makes it work.

One free way to find providers by ZIP code and specialty is the [NPPES NPI Registry](https://npiregistry.cms.hhs.gov/), the federal database of every U.S. health care provider's National Provider Identifier. It lists practice addresses and specialties. Use it for professional contact details only.

## 2. Sort by priority

Not everyone deserves the same effort. Give each name a letter:

- **A:** a strong fit for your specialty, close by, and a group or clinic that sees a lot of patients. These get personal outreach.
- **B:** a good fit with less volume, or a little farther away. These get email and quarterly updates.
- **C:** worth knowing about. These get your one-pager and an occasional update.

Aim for 10 to 15 A's. That's a number you can actually tend.

## 3. Set a cadence and stick to it

Relationships fade without contact, but nobody wants to hear from you every week. This rhythm works for most practices:

1. **Week 0, first touch.** A short, specific email or a dropped-off referral packet (see [How to get referrals from doctors](/blog/therapist-referrals-from-doctors/) for a script).
2. **Week 2, follow-up.** One brief note with something useful attached: a patient handout, your current availability.
3. **Month 1 to 2, a real conversation.** A 15-minute call, a coffee, or a short lunch-and-learn for their staff on a topic like screening for anxiety or talking to patients about therapy costs.
4. **Every quarter, an update.** Your openings, any new services, a new handout. Keep it to a few lines.
5. **After every referral, close the loop.** With the client's written consent, let the referrer know their patient connected. Without consent, a general thank-you with no client information.
6. **Once a year, a check-in.** Ask what's working for them, what their patients need, and whether anything has changed on their end.

Block 30 minutes on your calendar every other week for referral work. That small, regular habit beats an ambitious outreach push you abandon after a month.

## 4. Track it in one place

You don't need a CRM. A spreadsheet with these columns is enough. Keep it to professional contact details only, and never put client information in it.

| Priority | Name and credential | Specialty | Practice | Best contact | Why they're a fit | Status | Last touch | Next step | Next touch date | Referrals received | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Dr. Jane Doe, MD | Family medicine | Main Street Primary Care (6 providers) | Practice manager, Pat | Large panel, 1 mile away, sees many anxious young adults | Contacted | 2026-10-14 | Follow-up with patient handout | 2026-10-28 | 0 | Prefers email over calls |
| B | Sam Lee, PMHNP | Psychiatric NP | Riverside Psychiatry | Front desk | Needs therapists for medication patients | Met | 2026-09-30 | Quarterly update | 2026-12-30 | 2 | Two-way referrals |

Use a dropdown for Status so it stays consistent: Not contacted, Contacted, Met, Referring, Not a fit. The example rows are made up; replace them with your own.

Two numbers tell you whether the system is working: how many A-list contacts you've actually reached this quarter, and where your new clients come from. Ask every new client "How did you hear about me?" at intake and log the source (not the person) by month.

## 5. Give before you ask

The strongest networks run both ways. Refer clients to colleagues when they're the better fit. Share a resource a physician's patients would find useful. Offer a free 30-minute talk to a practice's staff. None of this is payment for referrals, which the ACA Code doesn't allow; it's being a useful professional neighbor.

## What a lasting network looks like a year from now

A handful of providers who know exactly who to send you, a few colleagues you trade referrals with, and a spreadsheet that tells you who you haven't talked to in a while. That's it. It isn't glamorous, but it's the most reliable source of good-fit clients a practice can build.
